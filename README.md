# youtube-conspiracy-engagement-

Conspiracy theories have spread widely through social media, with YouTube as a key example. While most research studies focus on viewers, we focus on content creators. YouTube usually shares ad revenue with creators, so videos that get more views or engagement tend to earn more. We want to know if this reward encourages channels to post more conspiracy theories. In a sample of over 3,160 labeled videos from the YouNICon dataset, conspiracy videos received about 45% more views than other videos from the same channel in the same year. However, these videos did not get more likes or comments per view. To expand on this dataset, we plan to label more channels that have posted at least one conspiracy video and track each channel over time. We will look at when they first posted conspiracy content and compare their views and engagement before and after. Next, we will test whether channels post more conspiracy videos after seeing closer attention on these videos. We will also examine whether this pattern changed after YouTube’s 2019 policy update, when YouTube adjusted its algorithm to stop recommending conspiracy videos.
